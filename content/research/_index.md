## <span style="background-color: lightyellow;"><span style="font-size:22px">A foreword on our group culture...</span></span>

We want our research group to be a **welcoming and inclusive safe place to work, learn and do science**. We value people with different backgrounds, identities, experiences and perspectives, and welcome students, researchers and collaborators **irrespective of who they are or where they come from.** 

Everyone joining the group is expected to contribute to a respectful, supportive and collegial environment. This means treating others fairly, listening to different perspectives, communicating with consideration, and helping create a space where people feel able to ask questions, share ideas, make mistakes and develop as scientists. **Discrimination, harassment and exclusionary behaviour have no place in our group.**

## <span style="background-color: lightyellow;"><span style="font-size:22px">Research themes (brief)</span></span>

Our research combines **microbiology, microbial (meta)genomics and bioinformatics to analyse large-scale human gut microbiome population cohorts** and study host-microbe associations, microbe-microbe interactions and microbial function in human health and disease. 

We use statistical genetics, systems genomics and ecological modelling to integrate clinical, genomic and multi-omics data and investigate **how commensals, pathobionts and the broader gut microbiota contribute to cardiometabolic, infectious, liver and other complex diseases**, with a potential interest to translation toward precision medicine and microbiome-targeted interventions. 

Our group works as part of consortia which include **Nordic population cohorts** and other publicly available large datasets. We also have an interest in larger biobank-scale cohorts to contextualise our research.

Our scientific interests have always been generalist and fundamental at heart, but a few topics are of particular current interest, through led projects in the lab or collaborations:

1) **Population and systems genomics of clinical, environmental and veterinary bacterial pathogens**, including evolution, transmission and host adaptation.
2) **Human microbiome epidemiology in large cohorts**, spanning metagenomics, metagenome-assembled genomes and multi-omics integration to map microbiome–host–environment interactions.
3) **Links between the gut microbiota, host genetics, lifestyle, diet and disease risk or progression in cardiometabolic and related conditions**
4) **Effect of antibiotic and non-antibiotic medication** on the gut microbiome and gut resistome dynamics.
5) Exploring the **human gut archaeome** and its link with metabolic health

We are always extremely open to collaboration. If you are interested, please get in touch.

## <span style="background-color: lightyellow;"><span style="font-size:22px">Research themes (detailed)</span></span>

<span style="font-size:18px">**The gut microbiome as a predictive factor for future health.**</span><br>We examine the question of whether microbes that we carry today can help us understand our health years from now. We link population-scale gut metagenomes with long-term health records. Our work identified microbial signatures associated with subsequent mortality (<b>Salosensaari <i>et al.</i>, 2021</b>) and type 2 diabetes (T2D; <b>Ruuskanen <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>), demonstrating that microbiome variation can contain information about future health well before disease is diagnosed. We have also observed that combining microbial profiles with conventional risk factors can improve prediction of incident liver disease (<b>Liu <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>) and chronic respiratory disease (<b>Liu <i>et al.</i>, 2023</b>), as well as several common diseases when combined with polygenic risk (<b>Liu <i>et al.</i>, 2024b</b>). Our interest is in understanding when the microbiome provides useful additional information, which microbial features underpin that information, and how these findings might eventually support earlier prevention of disease (<b>Liu <i>et al.</i>, 2024a</b>).

<span style="font-size:18px">**Human genetics and the ecology of the gut microbiome.**</span><br> Some microbes thrive in some people more than others. In this theme, we are interested in how human genetic variation can shape the intestinal microenvironment, and how its effects interact with diet and microbial metabolism. In large Nordic datasets, we identified host-microbe associations involving lactase persistence and dairy consumption along with evidence that blood type-related molecules could shape nutrient availability in the gut (<b>Qin <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>). We extended these observations in larger population cohorts, identifying and replicating associations connecting microbial variation with a range of other processes (SCFA sensing, mucus composition, bile acid biology, etc.) (<b>Dekkers <i>et al.</i>, 2026</b>, <b>Ohlsson <i>et al.</i>, 2026</b>). All of this supports an ecological view of host genetics: human variants can influence the habitats and resources available to microbes. We are interested in using these relationships to uncover and validate more mechanisms of host-microbe interactions, and clarify or confirm their relevance to health.

<span style="font-size:18px">**Diet, lifestyle and microbial function.**</span><br> Our gut microbiome sits at the interface between everyday exposures and human physiology. Through population-based collaborations, we investigate how dietary patterns and lifestyle relate to microbial communities and their functional potential. Healthier, plant- and fibre-rich food choices are associated with microbial profiles enriched in fibre-degrading and SCFA -producing organisms and functions (<b>Koponen <i>et al.</i>, 2021</b>). More detailed dietary analyses revealed distinct associations of plant-based foods, dairy and meat consumption with individual species and microbial functions (<b>Maukonen <i>et al.</i>, 2024</b>). We have also helped link alcohol consumption with microbial profiles consistent with greater inflammatory potential and explored their relationship with subsequent liver disease (<b>Koponen <i>et al.</i>, 2025</b>). Our interest extends to combinations of lifestyle factors, including those associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) risk (<b>Tammi <i>et al.</i>, 2026</b>).

<span style="font-size:18px">**Medication, microbial disruption and antibiotic resistance.**</span><br> Medicines interact with a complex microbial ecosystem, with consequences that may extend beyond their immediate therapeutic effects. Our international collaborative work has linked antibiotic prescriptions to differences in gut microbiome composition years after exposure with marked variation between antibiotic classes (<b>Baldanzi <i>et al.</i>, 2026</b>). We also investigate the gut resistome—the collection of antibiotic resistance genes carried by intestinal microbes. In FINRISK, resistance-gene burden was associated with antibiotic exposure, demographic and lifestyle factors, and subsequent mortality and sepsis risk (<b>Pärnänen <i>et al.</i>, 2025</b>); complementary work examined microbiome and resistome responses to treatment in elderly long-term-care residents (<b>Kitamura <i>et al.</i>, 2024</b>). Beyond antibiotics, we have explored associations between gut microbes and incident diabetes among statin users (<b>Koponen <i>et al.</i>, 2024</b>). Together, these studies motivate our interest in how medication exposure, microbial ecology and resistance interact to influence health.

<span style="font-size:18px">**The gut microbiome and blood pressure.** </span><br>Through collaborations in hypertension research in Australia, we investigate how gut microbes and their metabolites may contribute to BP regulation, and how this knowledge could inform prevention or treatment. Our synthesis of the field brought together evidence connecting microbial metabolism, intestinal barrier function, inflammation and established blood-pressure-regulating systems, while identifying challenges in translating these mechanisms into human interventions (<b>O'Donnell <i>et al.</i>, 2023</b>, <b>Muralitharan <i>et al.</i>, 2023</b>). We have also tested the predictive contribution of gut microbiome data alongside genetics, circulating metabolites and clinical measurements. In a long-term population study, microbiome data did not improve hypertension prediction beyond clinical risk factors, while polygenic scores provided a small additional benefit (<b>Vuori <i>et al.</i>, 2026</b>). This theme connects mechanistic questions with careful evaluation of practical utility: understanding microbial involvement in hypertension biology and establishing where that understanding can improve care.

<span style="font-size:18px">**Reliable methods for understanding microbial communities.**</span><br> We're always interested in supporting work that focuses on better methods to understand microbiome biology and metagenomics. We regularly contribute to computational and methodological approaches that make microbial variation more interpretable across large datasets. Collaborative work on genome-based, phylogeny-aware analysis has shown how evolutionary relationships can reveal biologically meaningful patterns that conventional taxonomic summaries may overlook (<b>Zhu <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>). We were also among the first to establish a GTDB-based metagenomic classification pipeline (<b>Méric, Wick <i>et al.</i>, 2019</b>). We have also evaluated how sample handling and other technical factors distort microbial profiles, a  nd how unwanted variation can be reduced computationally (<b>Fachrul <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>). Contributions to study design guidance extend this interest to experimental and clinical microbiome research (<b>Muralitharan <i>et al.</i>, 2023</b>). Our aim is to develop and apply approaches that help distinguish biological signals from measurement artefacts and support reproducible discoveries.

<span style="font-size:18px">**Earlier work: bacterial evolution, host adaptation and pathogenicity.**</span><br> Our earlier research examined how bacterial genomes evolve as organisms adapt to different hosts and move between commensal carriage and disease. In <i>Campylobacter</i>, we linked host specificity to metabolic adaptation and showed how shared host environments facilitate genetic exchange between species (<b>Mourkas <i>et al.</i>, 2022</b>). In opportunistic pathogens, we combined population genomics, association studies and laboratory microbiology to identify disease-associated genetic variation in the skin commensal <i>Staphylococcus epidermidis</i> (<b>Méric, Mageiros <i>et al.</i>, 2018</b>) and avian <i>Escherichia coli</i> (<b>Mageiros <i>et al.</i>, 2021</b>), showing how horizontal gene transfer can distribute pathogenicity-associated traits across divergent lineages. This work established a continuing interest in microbial diversity, ecological adaptation and the context-dependent boundary between harmless colonisation and disease.
